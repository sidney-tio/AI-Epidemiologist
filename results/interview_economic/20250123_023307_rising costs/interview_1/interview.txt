Interviewee: Good day to you too. I am Hiroshi Tanaka and I have been working as a farmer for the past 20 years. Interviewer: Thank you for sharing, Mr. Tanaka. Your commitment to your craft and community is truly commendable. Let's delve a bit deeper into the challenges you mentioned. What do you think about the current situation of rising costs in Japan's domestic agriculture?

Interviewee: Rising costs in domestic agriculture are indeed a significant challenge. In recent years, we have seen an increase in the cost of farm inputs such as fertilizers, seeds, and machinery. Also, as younger generations move to cities, labor becomes scarce and more expensive in rural areas.

As farmers, we're at the mercy of these rising costs, yet our selling prices don't always reflect this dynamic. This imbalance puts a strain on our finances and makes it increasingly difficult to maintain profitability.

Furthermore, these higher costs are pushing many farmers to either scale up their operations to lower per unit costs, or to abandon farming altogether. For small, traditional farmers like us, scaling up isn't always an option due to limited resources and land. This is a matter of great concern, not just for us farmers, but for the future of domestic agriculture in Japan.

Interviewer: It's clear that these financial pressures are significant. On top of that, there's the influx of higher quality and cheaper products from abroad. How do you believe this is affecting local farmers like yourself?

Interviewee: Imported agricultural products pose another layer of challenge for us. These products are often cheaper and, in some cases, of comparable or even superior quality. With the increasing globalization and free trade agreements, the influx of these products into the Japanese market has increased.

For local farmers, this means we are competing not only with our fellow Japanese farmers, but also with farmers from all over the world. Consumers naturally gravitate towards products that offer the best value, and this often means choosing imported goods over our locally grown produce.

Yet, I believe there is more to the story than just price and quality. Our rice, for example, is grown with care, using traditional methods that have been passed down for generations. It's more than just a crop; it's a reflection of our culture and heritage. It’s important to communicate this value to consumers and help them understand the significance and worth of locally grown produce. 

While we cannot control the global market forces, we can focus on what sets us apart – our local knowledge, our dedication to quality, and our commitment to preserving our traditional farming methods. These are our strengths, and we must leverage them to compete and survive in this challenging environment.

Interviewer: Your passion for your work and dedication to your heritage is evident, Mr. Tanaka. Given these challenges, what measures do you think could be taken to support local farmers in this demanding environment?

Interviewee: There are several measures that could provide much-needed support to local farmers. Firstly, the government can play a significant role by offering subsidies or financial assistance to offset the rising costs of farming. This could help us maintain profitability and continue our operations.

Secondly, investment in agricultural research and development can lead to improvements in farming techniques and equipment, which would increase our productivity and efficiency. It's also crucial to explore sustainable farming methods that can help us protect our environment while meeting our production needs.

Thirdly, there's a need for initiatives that educate consumers about the value and quality of locally grown produce. Encouraging consumers to buy local can significantly boost the demand for our products. In this regard, I believe we farmers also have a role to play by participating in community outreach programs and farmers' markets to directly connect with consumers.

Lastly, empowering the next generation is crucial. Providing agricultural education and creating attractive career opportunities in farming can encourage young people to stay in rural areas. This would help address the labor shortage and ensure the continuity of our farming traditions.

All these measures require a combined effort from the government, the community, and us farmers. It's a challenging journey, but I believe that with resilience and cooperation, we can navigate through these difficulties and secure the future of local agriculture in Japan.
